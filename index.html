Microbial life & deathin complex environments

Microbes do not live in isolation in flasks or on agar plates. Instead, their natural environments are crowded 3D spaces like host tissues, soils, and marine habitats, where they form spatially structured communities, compete or cooperate with other species, and face stressors such as bacteriophages. Our lab seeks to understand how bacteria navigate these complex environments, combining live imaging in engineered microbial habitats with molecular biology, theory, and computation to predict, and ultimately control, how microbial communities withstand phages, antibiotics, and competitors.

About the lab — Overarching goal

How microbial communities self-organize, interact, and withstand stressors in complex environments

Our overarching goal is to investigate how bacteria interact with their natural stressors, such as phages and antibiotics, and with other microbial species in complex 3D environments, ultimately bridging the gap between laboratory settings and real-life environments.

Bacteria inhabit almost every ecosystem on Earth, from soils and bodies of water to industrial processes and our own bodies, with major consequences for biogeochemistry, ecology, agriculture, and health. In these habitats they rarely live in isolation in a well-mixed liquid. Instead, they form dense, spatially structured communities inside crowded 3D spaces such as soil pores, sediments, mucus, and tissues, where nutrients run short, neighbors compete or cooperate, and phages, the viruses that infect bacteria and outnumber them about ten to one, are never far away.

This spatial organization shapes community growth, stability, cross-feeding, and diversity. Yet most of what we know about bacteria comes from single species in well-mixed cultures or flat colonies on agar plates, which fail to capture the spatial arrangement and composition of communities in nature. To close this gap, we engineer transparent 3D environments that mimic soils and host tissues, image communities in them from single cells to whole colonies, and develop theory that helps us understand the key experimental observations.

Two overarching themes to bridge the gap

Stressors Phages and antibiotics How do bacterial communities withstand phages and antibiotics? We study how anti-phage defense systems protect structured communities, and search for the combinations, doses, and protocols of phages and antibiotics that eradicate them, with direct implications for phage therapy. Multispecies communities Bacteria and phytoplankton How do species that interact metabolically organize, cooperate, or compete in space? We study consortia of bloom-forming cyanobacteria and heterotrophic bacteria, and how phages reshape them.

Stressors

How do bacterial communities withstand phages and antibiotics?

Anti-phage bacterial defenseFight or surrender: which defenses protect crowded communities?

Bacteria carry a large repertoire of anti-phage defense systems that prevent phage infection from spreading in a community. Some, such as restriction–modification and CRISPR–Cas, target the phage DNA upon entry into the cell; the latter also provides adaptive immunity. A different class, abortive infection (Abi) systems, acts as an altruistic last-resort strategy in which infected cells trigger programmed cell death before new phages are released, protecting their neighbors.

We want to understand:

  • Risk. Which defenses are riskier for the community, and under which environmental and physiological conditions?
  • Expression and autoimmunity. When and where are defense systems expressed within a community, and when do they turn against the cell itself?
  • Mixed populations. What role does cellular heterogeneity play in protecting the community?

Phage & antibiotic cocktailsCan we design optimal phage–antibiotic cocktails to eradicate 3D bacterial populations?

Phage therapy is one of the most promising strategies against antimicrobial resistance. In the clinic, phages are still used mostly as a compassionate treatment once antibiotics have failed. We want to discover which combinations of phages and antibiotics act in synergy, optimize their dose and protocol, follow how resistance evolves, and understand how a complex environment and a structured 3D population change the outcome.

Multispecies communities

How do metabolically coupled species organize and survive together?

Trophic interactionsWhat turns a microbial partnership into a harmful bloom, and can phages stop it?

Harmful algal blooms foul drinking water and fisheries, and some release toxins. No bloom grows alone: bloom-forming cyanobacteria release dissolved organic matter and oxygen that feed heterotrophic bacteria, which return CO₂. We rebuild these consortia in transparent hydrogels and watch the partners organize in space. In our first experiments, heterotrophs are drawn to the cyanobacteria at low density; once the cyanobacteria reach a critical density they make their surroundings toxic and nearby bacterial populations collapse, much as dense blooms disrupt microbial ecosystems in nature.

  • Spatial organization. How does metabolite exchange position consumers around their producers, and what flips cooperation into collapse?
  • Bloom control. Can cyanophages collapse a bloom, and who feeds on what the lysed cells release?

Left: the filamentous, bloom-forming cyanobacterium Nostoc. Right: two bacterial populations sort into interlocking domains in a 3D hydrogel.

How we work

Experiments and theory, side by side

Every project couples engineered habitats, quantitative live imaging, and genetic and environmental perturbations with theory that makes testable predictions.

Build

We precisely 3D-print and manipulate microbial communities and stressors in transparent packings of hydrogel microparticles or glass beads.

Image

Time-lapse microscopy of whole 3D communities over days, from single cells to entire colonies.

Perturb

We challenge communities with phages and antibiotics, vary nutrients and oxygen, and tune gene expression to dissect how they respond.

Model

Continuum and agent-based models, grounded in soft-matter physics and statistical mechanics, that explain our observations and guide new experiments.

Alejandro Martínez-Calvo

Principal Investigator

Alejandro Martínez-Calvo is an Associate Research Scholar and fellow of the Center for the Physics of Biological Function at Princeton University, in the Departments of Physics and Molecular Biology, and the Lewis-Sigler Institute for Integrative Genomics. In 2027 he will open his lab as a Principal Ramón y Cajal Investigator in the Department of Systems Biology at the National Biotechnology Center (CNB-CSIC) in Madrid.

Alex earned a BSc in Engineering (2015) and an MSc in Applied Mathematics (2017) from Universidad Carlos III de Madrid. He stayed there for a PhD in Fluid Mechanics with Alejandro Sevilla (supported by the FPU program), studying how liquid threads and films with complex interfaces and rheology thin and break up, and received the Extraordinary PhD Award. In 2021 he moved to Princeton as a postdoc supported by the Human Frontier Science Program and the Princeton Center for Theoretical Science fellows program, where he worked with Ned Wingreen, Sujit Datta, and Zemer Gitai.
